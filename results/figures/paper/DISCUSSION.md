# Results Discussion And Conclusion

This note is written in a paper-ready style and is intended to accompany the figures in this directory.

## Suggested Results Narrative

Across the full matrix, the learned detectors substantially outperformed the classical baselines in aggregate AP50, but that advantage depended strongly on how much site shift was present. As summarized in `paper_method_mean_ap50.png`, Faster R-CNN achieved the strongest overall mean AP50 at `80.6%`, followed closely by YOLO26 at `79.4%`. U-Net formed a clear second tier at `67.5%`, while the best classical methods clustered tightly below that level: Otsu at `57.6%`, OS-CFAR at `56.6%`, and CA-CFAR at `55.4%`. SS-Otsu fell further back at `46.6%`, RT-DETR collapsed to `13.6%`, and the wavelet detector was effectively non-competitive at `3.9%`.

Condition-wise, `paper_ap50_by_condition_families.png` shows that Faster R-CNN was the most reliable method across all three evaluation settings. It led the pooled runs (`95.9%` AP50), the site-permutation runs (`71.5%`), and the transfer runs (`78.1%`), with YOLO26 consistently close behind (`94.2%`, `71.5%`, and `76.4%`, respectively). U-Net remained clearly stronger than any classical method on average, especially in pooled evaluation (`80.5%` AP50), but it lost more performance under site shift than Faster R-CNN and YOLO26. Among the classical methods, Otsu, OS-CFAR, and CA-CFAR formed a relatively tight cluster, suggesting that in this dataset the best non-learned methods are all viable but ceiling-limited alternatives rather than fundamentally different performance tiers.

The AP75 results sharpen this interpretation. `paper_ap75_by_condition_families.png` and `paper_localization_retention.png` show that the best detectors were not only finding targets, but localizing them precisely. YOLO26 and Faster R-CNN retained nearly all of their AP50 at the stricter AP75 threshold (`98.9%` and `98.5%` AP75/AP50 retention), and OS-CFAR also retained `99.0%`, indicating that when it fired, its box geometry was usually well aligned with the target support. U-Net retained only `68.1%`, consistent with its mask-to-box conversion stage producing boxes that were often close but not tight. Wavelet retained only `0.4%`, confirming that its weak AP50 was not merely a thresholding problem: it was failing both to separate clutter and to localize detections accurately.

## Generalization And Transfer

The transfer figures reveal that domain shift, not pooled-data fitting, is the central challenge in this problem. `paper_transfer_heatmaps_all_methods.png` and `paper_transfer_generalization_gap.png` show the same pattern across most of the successful methods: strong on-diagonal performance and a marked drop off-diagonal. The cross-site gap was largest for the strongest learned models, with Faster R-CNN losing `26.6` AP50 points from same-site transfer to cross-site transfer and YOLO26 losing `25.9` points. U-Net showed a similar but slightly smaller loss (`23.1` points). The classical methods exhibited smaller gaps, but that should not be interpreted as stronger robustness. Their ceiling was lower to begin with, so there was less same-site headroom to lose.

One result is especially consistent: DARC was the hardest transfer target for nearly every competitive method. `paper_transfer_target_site_breakdown.png` shows that cross-site AP50 on DARC dropped to `22.8%` for CA-CFAR, `29.0%` for OS-CFAR, `39.3%` for Otsu, `48.4%` for U-Net, `60.5%` for Faster R-CNN, and `63.8%` for YOLO26. By contrast, MANV and DAAN were much more recoverable. This suggests that the dominant transfer failure is not symmetric site shift, but a particular mismatch between the DARC target distribution and the statistics learned or tuned on MANV and DAAN.

The pairwise transfer breakdown makes the asymmetry even clearer. For the learned models, the hardest direction was consistently `MANV -> DARC`: U-Net achieved only `22.9%` AP50, Faster R-CNN `25.5%`, and YOLO26 `34.7%`, compared with same-site transfer values above `84%` for U-Net and above `95%` for Faster R-CNN and YOLO26. These are not mild degradations. They indicate that the strongest models are learning site-dependent priors that do not survive the hardest deployment condition without adaptation.

## Failure Analysis

The failure contact sheets show that the transfer degradation is not just a scalar drop in score; it changes the qualitative error mode. In `paper_failure_hard_transfer_mvdc.png`, the `MANV -> DARC` examples are dominated by sparse bright streaks and clustered small responses. U-Net often places boxes near the dominant structures but fails to cover the full set of targets. Faster R-CNN recovers more positives, but in the hardest frames it also generates several elongated false positives along clutter streaks. YOLO26 is generally the most balanced of the three, but even it misses part of the dense cluster structure in the most difficult DARC scenes. The common pattern is that all three methods struggle when target-like streaks are embedded in site-specific clutter geometry rather than isolated point targets.

`paper_failure_rtdetr_collapse_dcmv.png` highlights a different failure regime. RT-DETR did not merely underperform; it often collapsed into high-volume low-quality prediction. In the illustrated `DARC -> MANV` examples it emitted `300` predictions per image while matching `0` ground-truth boxes, whereas YOLO26 remained sparse (`9` to `13` predictions) and matched all or nearly all targets. Together with `paper_detection_volume_by_condition.png`, this shows that RT-DETR’s poor AP50 was coupled to a severe calibration failure rather than a conservative miss-only regime. The model was not learning a usable objectness threshold on this dataset.

## Ablation And Winner Stability

The winner-count figures suggest that the final conclusions are not an artifact of unstable hyperparameter search. Wavelet was the clearest example of saturation: `paper_wavelet_winner_parameter_counts.png` shows level `2` was selected in all `20/20` experiments and `sigma=7.5` in `18/20`, yet the method remained non-competitive. This supports the interpretation that wavelet underperformance is structural rather than a consequence of insufficient tuning.

The same stability appears, in a more useful way, for the better methods. `paper_unet_winner_parameter_counts.png` shows that `max_area=4000`, `dilation=0`, and `box_padding=0` were selected in all `20/20` U-Net experiments, while the only meaningful residual search variance was in the threshold and minimum-area settings. The SS-Otsu winner plots also show a concentrated optimum around block size `96`, seed threshold factor `1.75`, and grow threshold `0.8`, which argues that the classical baselines were tuned in a stable region rather than on isolated lucky trials.

The seed and site-permutation figures reinforce that point. `paper_pooled_seed_variability.png` shows that the top methods were not excessively sensitive to random pooled splits, while `paper_site_permutation_ap50_families.png` shows that the site ordering changes were meaningful primarily when they changed which domain the model had to generalize into. In other words, the dominant uncertainty source in this benchmark is site shift, not training noise.

## Practical Interpretation

If the objective is the strongest detector under the existing splits, Faster R-CNN is the most defensible choice. It had the highest overall AP50, the highest pooled AP50, the highest transfer AP50, and near-perfect AP75 retention. YOLO26 is a credible alternative when slight performance loss is acceptable in exchange for a different deployment profile, but in this study it did not clearly surpass Faster R-CNN on any major aggregate criterion. U-Net is attractive as a lighter learned baseline and may still be valuable if pixel masks are of independent interest, but its localization drop indicates that its current postprocessing is leaving performance on the table.

Among the classical methods, Otsu, OS-CFAR, and CA-CFAR remain useful reference points because they are much cheaper to evaluate than the learned detectors and they fail more gracefully than wavelet or RT-DETR. Otsu is especially notable because its mean AP50 (`57.6%`) was only modestly ahead of the CFAR variants, yet its experiment runtime was far lower than OS-CFAR and SS-Otsu. That makes it a strong classical baseline even though it is not a contender for best overall performance.

The negative results are also informative. The wavelet detector is not a viable path for this dataset in its current form, and RT-DETR appears mismatched to the data regime and supervision available here. Both methods should be treated as lessons about model-task fit rather than just under-tuned baselines.

## Conclusion

The main conclusion is that learning-based object detectors can outperform classical radar detectors by a wide margin on this corpus, but they do not eliminate the domain-shift problem. The best methods, Faster R-CNN and YOLO26, were excellent on pooled and same-site evaluations and still clearly strongest under transfer, yet both suffered substantial asymmetric degradation on the hardest cross-site directions, especially when testing on DARC. Classical baselines remained competitive only as lower-cost, lower-ceiling alternatives, and wavelet-based detection was not supported by the evidence.

For a journal paper, the most defensible framing is therefore not simply that learned models are better than classical ones. The stronger claim is that the benchmark separates three different questions: raw in-domain accuracy, localization quality, and transfer robustness. Faster R-CNN answered all three most convincingly, YOLO26 was a close second, U-Net showed that dense prediction is promising but postprocessing-limited, and the classical methods remain important because they reveal how much of the task can be solved by local statistics alone. The remaining open problem is robust cross-site generalization, not in-domain detection capacity.
